\chapter{Input Models: State Preparation and Block Encoding}
\label{ch:lec06}

\begin{objectives}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,nosep]
  \item Describe the state-preparation oracle as the \emph{vector} input model and
        state when it can be implemented efficiently.
  \item Define block encoding as the \emph{matrix} input model, and read off the
        subnormalization factor $\alpha$, ancilla count $a$, and error $\epsilon$.
  \item Apply a block encoding to a prepared state to perform
        \emph{matrix--vector multiplication}, and read its cost off $\alpha$ and the
        output norm.
  \item Combine input models by \emph{matrix addition}, \emph{vector addition}, and
        \emph{matrix--matrix multiplication}, tracking how $\alpha$ and the ancilla
        count compose.
  \item Construct block encodings of $1$-sparse and $s$-sparse matrices from
        entry and location oracles.
  \item Distinguish Hermitian from general block encodings and explain why the
        distinction matters for qubitization.
  \item Recognize the block-encoding relation in the general projector form
        $A=\alpha\,\Pi'\,U_A\,\Pi$, valid beyond the computational basis.
\end{itemize}
\end{objectives}

Part~I treated quantum states and unitary circuits as the primitive objects.
Part~II turns to \emph{quantum numerical linear algebra}: solving linear systems,
applying matrix functions, simulating dynamics. Every such task begins with the
same question---the \emph{input model}: how does a matrix $A\in\C^{N\times N}$
(with $N=2^n$), which is generally \emph{not} unitary and may be dense, enter a
quantum computer, whose only native operations are unitary? This lecture develops
the two answers that underpin the entire QNLA toolbox. The first, the
\emph{state-preparation oracle}, loads a classical vector into the amplitudes of a
quantum state and is the input model for the right-hand side of a linear system.
The second, \emph{block encoding}, embeds $A$ (rescaled) as the top-left corner of
a larger unitary $U_A$; it is the input model for the matrix itself and the single
abstraction on which linear combination of unitaries (LCU, \cref{ch:lec07}),
qubitization and quantum signal processing (QSP, \cref{ch:lec08}), the quantum
singular value transformation (QSVT, \cref{ch:lec09}), and quantum linear-system
solvers (\cref{ch:lec10}) are all built. We give the definition, quantify its cost
with the \emph{subnormalization factor} $\alpha$, and construct block encodings of
sparse matrices explicitly from a pair of oracles---one for entry values, one for
nonzero locations. Throughout, $A$ is an $n$-qubit matrix and
$\norm{A}$ denotes the spectral (operator) norm.

\section{State-preparation oracles}
\label{sec:lec06-1}

Many algorithms are posed relative to a fixed input vector: the linear-system
problem $Ax=b$ needs the right-hand side $b$; ground-state energy estimation
(\cref{sec:lec05-1}) needs an initial guess $\ket{\phi}$; a Monte Carlo estimand is
encoded in a prepared amplitude (\cref{sec:lec05-2}). In each case the vector must
be loaded into the amplitudes of an $n$-qubit register. This is the vector input
model.

\begin{definition}[State-preparation oracle]
\label{def:lec06-sp}
Let $u\in\C^{N}$ be a nonzero vector with normalized version
$\ket{u}=u/\norm{u}_2=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}u_i\ket{i}/\norm{u}_2$. A
\emph{state-preparation oracle} for $u$ is an $n$-qubit unitary $O_u$ with
\begin{equation}
  O_u\ket{0^n}=\ket{u}.
  \label{eq:lec06-sp}
\end{equation}
The oracle is \emph{efficient} if $O_u$ is implemented by a circuit of size
$\poly(n)=\polylog(N)$.
\end{definition}

Only the direction of $u$ survives normalization; the norm $\norm{u}_2$ is a
classical scalar that must be tracked separately (it reappears, for example, as the
scaling of the solution of a linear system). The essential subtlety is that
\eqref{eq:lec06-sp} is trivial to \emph{write down} but not always cheap to
\emph{implement}.

\begin{proposition}[Cost of exact amplitude loading]
\label{prop:lec06-sp}
For any $\ket{u}\in\C^{N}$ there exists a state-preparation unitary $O_u$
satisfying \eqref{eq:lec06-sp} using $\bigo(N)=\bigo(2^n)$ elementary gates and no
ancilla qubits. In general this cost cannot be reduced below $\Omega(2^n/n)$ gates
for a worst-case $\ket{u}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Prepare $\ket{u}$ by a sequence of controlled single-qubit rotations, one per qubit,
that build up the amplitudes conditionally on the bits already set. Concretely, the
first qubit is rotated so that the probabilities of its two branches match the total
weight of $u$ on states with first bit $0$ versus $1$; conditioned on that bit, the
second qubit is rotated to split the weight again; and so on. Level $k$ of this tree
requires $2^{k-1}$ controlled rotations (one per branch), so the total is
$\sum_{k=1}^{n}2^{k-1}=2^{n}-1=\bigo(N)$ rotations, each with phase and magnitude
angles read off from partial sums of $\abs{u_i}^2$ and $\arg u_i$. For the lower
bound, a general $\ket{u}$ carries $\Theta(2^n)$ independent real parameters, while a
circuit of $g$ two-qubit gates on $n$ qubits has only $\bigo(g)$ continuous
parameters; matching the parameter counts forces $g=\Omega(2^n/n)$.
\end{proof}

The message of \cref{prop:lec06-sp} is that \emph{arbitrary} amplitude loading is
exponentially expensive and destroys any hope of a quantum speedup on its own.
Efficient state preparation is possible only for \emph{structured} vectors.

\begin{example}[When state preparation is efficient]
\label{exa:lec06-spgood}
Four broad structures admit $\polylog(N)$-size preparation.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,nosep]
  \item \emph{Product (unentangled) states.} If $\ket{u}=\bigotimes_{k=1}^{n}
        \ket{v_k}$ with each $\ket{v_k}$ a single qubit, then $O_u=\bigotimes_k
        R_k$ is $n$ single-qubit rotations. The uniform superposition
        $\Hgate^{\otimes n}\ket{0^n}$ is the special case $\ket{v_k}=\ket{+}$.
  \item \emph{Efficiently integrable distributions.} If $\abs{u_i}^2=p(i)$ for a
        probability density $p$ whose cumulative distribution is classically
        computable, the Grover--Rudolph procedure loads $\ket{u}$ with $\bigo(n)$
        controlled rotations, each rotation angle set from an integral of $p$ over a
        dyadic subinterval.
  \item \emph{Sparse vectors.} If $u$ has only $d=\poly(n)$ nonzero entries whose
        indices and values are known, $\ket{u}$ can be prepared with $\bigo(dn)$
        gates by loading the $d$ amplitudes one at a time.
  \item \emph{Matrix product states.} If $\ket{u}$ is a matrix product state (an
        $\mathrm{MPS}$, i.e.\ a one-dimensional tensor network) of bond dimension
        $\chi$, it can be prepared \emph{exactly} by $n$ sequential unitaries, each
        acting on $\lceil\log_2\chi\rceil+1$ qubits---$\bigo(n\,\poly(\chi))$ gates in
        all. The product-state bullet above is the $\chi=1$ special case. States of
        bounded bond dimension are the subject of Part~IV (\cref{ch:lec15}); the
        higher-dimensional analogue (PEPS) is \emph{not} efficiently preparable in
        general.
\end{itemize}
When no such structure is available one may fall back on quantum random-access
memory (QRAM), which supplies $O_u$ in $\bigo(n)$ \emph{depth} at the price of
$\bigo(N)$ hardware; whether QRAM is a realistic resource is a standing debate.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[The Grover--Rudolph procedure]
\label{rem:lec06-gr}
The second class above is the workhorse for loading probability distributions, so it
is worth spelling out. Suppose the target has real nonnegative amplitudes
$\ket{u}=\sum_{i}\sqrt{p(i)}\,\ket{i}$, the discretization of a density $p$ on
$[0,1)$ to the $2^{n}$ dyadic cells (the index $i$ is the binary fraction
$0.i_1i_2\cdots i_n$). Grover--Rudolph builds $\ket{u}$ qubit by qubit, splitting the
probability mass as it descends. Once the first $k$ bits are fixed they name a dyadic
interval $I$ of width $2^{-k}$; the rotation applied to qubit $k{+}1$ is
\[
  R_y(\theta_I)\ket0=\cos\tfrac{\theta_I}{2}\ket0+\sin\tfrac{\theta_I}{2}\ket1,
  \qquad
  \cos^{2}\tfrac{\theta_I}{2}=\frac{\int_{I_{\mathrm{left}}}p}{\int_{I}p},
\]
so the two branches inherit exactly the masses of the left and right halves of $I$.
Telescoping over all $n$ levels, the squared amplitude on $\ket i$ equals the cell
mass $\int_{\text{cell }i}p\approx p(i)$, as required.

The point is \emph{why this costs only $\bigo(n)$ rotations}. Hard-coding a separate
angle for each of the $2^{k-1}$ intervals at level $k$ would cost
$\sum_{k=1}^{n}2^{k-1}=\bigo(2^{n})$---no better than the generic
\cref{prop:lec06-sp}. The gain rests entirely on the hypothesis that the integrals
$\int_{I}p$ are \emph{classically efficiently computable}: instead of enumerating
intervals, one holds the already-set bits $i_1\cdots i_k$ in a register, computes the
angle $\theta$ from them \emph{coherently} (arithmetic on an ancilla using the
cumulative distribution of $p$), applies a rotation by that register-held angle, and
uncomputes. Each level is one such $\poly(n)$-gate block, and there are $n$ levels.
A caution (Herbert, 2021): if the integrals can only be \emph{estimated} by Monte
Carlo rather than evaluated in closed form, the sampling cost can cancel the very
speedup the loaded state was meant to deliver---so the method pays off only for
genuinely integrable densities (log-concave, Gaussian, and the like).
\end{remark}

We make each of the four classes concrete, with its preparation circuit.

\begin{example}[Product state]
\label{exa:lec06-prod}
Take the three-qubit product state
$\ket{u}=\ket+\otimes\ket0\otimes\bigl(\cos\tfrac\pi8\ket0+\sin\tfrac\pi8\ket1\bigr)$.
Because it factorizes, $O_u=\Hgate\otimes\id\otimes R_y(\tfrac\pi4)$ is one
single-qubit gate per wire (none on the middle qubit)---no two-qubit gate, hence no
entanglement.
\begin{center}
\begin{quantikz}
  \lstick{$\ket0$} & \gate{\Hgate}        & \qw \\
  \lstick{$\ket0$} & \qw                  & \qw \\
  \lstick{$\ket0$} & \gate{R_y(\pi/4)}    & \qw
\end{quantikz}
\end{center}
The $n$ amplitudes of a product state carry only $n$ independent angles, so $n$
rotations suffice; the uniform superposition $\Hgate^{\otimes n}\ket{0^n}$ is the case
in which every gate is a Hadamard.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Efficiently integrable: Grover--Rudolph on four points]
\label{exa:lec06-grex}
Load $p=(3,1,1,3)/8$ on $n=2$ qubits, i.e.\
$\ket{u}=\tfrac1{\sqrt8}\bigl(\sqrt3\,\ket{00}+\ket{01}+\ket{10}+\sqrt3\,\ket{11}\bigr)$.
With $q_1$ the most significant bit, the two halves $\{00,01\}$ and $\{10,11\}$ carry
equal mass $\tfrac12$, so the level-1 angle obeys $\cos^2(\theta_1/2)=\tfrac12$, i.e.\
$R_y(\pi/2)=\Hgate$ on $q_1$. Conditioned on $q_1$ the level-2 angle splits each half:
$q_1{=}0$ gives $\cos^2(\theta/2)=p_{00}/(p_{00}{+}p_{01})=\tfrac34$, so $R_y(\pi/3)$;
$q_1{=}1$ gives $p_{10}/(p_{10}{+}p_{11})=\tfrac14$, so $R_y(2\pi/3)$. Level~2 is a
\emph{uniformly controlled} rotation---one angle per value of $q_1$:
\begin{center}
\begin{quantikz}
  \lstick{$q_1:\ket0$} & \gate{\Hgate} & \octrl{1}          & \ctrl{1}            & \qw \\
  \lstick{$q_2:\ket0$} & \qw           & \gate{R_y(\pi/3)}  & \gate{R_y(2\pi/3)}  & \qw
\end{quantikz}
\end{center}
The amplitude on $\ket{00}$ is $\cos\tfrac\pi4\cos\tfrac\pi6
=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt3}2=\sqrt3/\sqrt8=\sqrt{p_{00}}$, as it must be. For a
smooth density the per-level angles are \emph{computed} from $\int_I p$ on an ancilla
rather than hard-coded, which is the content of \cref{rem:lec06-gr}.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Sparse vector]
\label{exa:lec06-sparsevec}
The three-qubit state $\ket{u}=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{000}+\ket{101})$ has only $d=2$
nonzero amplitudes. Equal weights on the two words are produced by one Hadamard and one
$\CNOT$: $\Hgate$ on $q_0$ makes $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{000}+\ket{100})$, and
$\CNOT_{q_0\to q_2}$ flips the last bit of the $\ket{1\cdots}$ branch to give
$\ket{101}$.
\begin{center}
\begin{quantikz}
  \lstick{$q_0:\ket0$} & \gate{\Hgate} & \ctrl{2} & \qw \\
  \lstick{$q_1:\ket0$} & \qw           & \qw      & \qw \\
  \lstick{$q_2:\ket0$} & \qw           & \targ{}  & \qw
\end{quantikz}
\end{center}
In general a $d$-sparse vector is loaded amplitude by amplitude: $\bigo(n)$ gates to
address each of the $d$ locations and set its magnitude and phase, for $\bigo(dn)$
total.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Matrix product state: GHZ]
\label{exa:lec06-mps}
The GHZ state $\ket{u}=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{000}+\ket{111})$ is the simplest nontrivial
matrix product state, of bond dimension $\chi=2$. Its sequential MPS preparation is the
textbook circuit---a Hadamard on the first qubit, then a $\CNOT$ chain carrying the bond
index along the register:
\begin{center}
\begin{quantikz}
  \lstick{$q_0:\ket0$} & \gate{\Hgate} & \ctrl{1} & \qw      & \qw \\
  \lstick{$q_1:\ket0$} & \qw           & \targ{}  & \ctrl{1} & \qw \\
  \lstick{$q_2:\ket0$} & \qw           & \qw      & \targ{}  & \qw
\end{quantikz}
\end{center}
Each gate acts on $\lceil\log_2\chi\rceil+1=2$ qubits and there are $n-1$ of them,
matching the $\bigo(n\,\poly(\chi))$ count; for larger $\chi$ the $\CNOT$s become the
bond unitaries of \cref{ch:lec15}.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Bond dimension for general $n$: GHZ ($\chi=2$) and Dicke ($\chi=3$)]
\label{rem:lec06-mpschi}
Both families extend to any number of qubits, the bond index carrying exactly the
information the left block must pass to the right across a cut.

\emph{Bond dimension $2$ (GHZ).} The $n$-qubit GHZ state
$\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0^{\otimes n}+\ket1^{\otimes n})$ is the MPS with the diagonal
$2\times2$ tensors $A^0=\proj0$, $A^1=\proj1$ and boundary covectors
$\bra{L}=\bra0+\bra1$, $\ket{R}=\ket0+\ket1$: the product $A^{x_1}\cdots A^{x_n}$
survives only when all bits agree (any mixed string contains a factor
$\proj0\proj1=0$), leaving the two uniform terms. The bond transmits a single bit
---which branch---so $\chi=2$. The sequential circuit is one $\Hgate$ followed by a
chain of $n-1$ nearest-neighbor $\CNOT$s (shown for $n=4$; the single ``carried''
qubit \emph{is} the bond):
\begin{center}
\begin{quantikz}
  \lstick{$q_1:\ket0$} & \gate{\Hgate} & \ctrl{1} & \qw      & \qw      & \qw \\
  \lstick{$q_2:\ket0$} & \qw           & \targ{}  & \ctrl{1} & \qw      & \qw \\
  \lstick{$q_3:\ket0$} & \qw           & \qw      & \targ{}  & \ctrl{1} & \qw \\
  \lstick{$q_4:\ket0$} & \qw           & \qw      & \qw      & \targ{}  & \qw
\end{quantikz}
\end{center}

\emph{Bond dimension $3$ (Dicke).} The weight-$2$ Dicke state
$\ket{D^n_2}=\binom n2^{-1/2}\sum_{\abs x=2}\ket x$ is the MPS whose bond index
$m\in\{0,1,2\}$ counts the $1$s placed so far, with the $3\times3$ tensors
\[
  A^0=\id_3,\qquad
  A^1=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\ 1&0&0\\ 0&1&0\end{pmatrix},
\]
read from the start bond $m=0$ to the end bond $m=2$. Because the $A^0$ factors are
identities, the product reduces to $(A^1)^{\abs x}$, and
$\bra{2}(A^1)^{\abs x}\ket0=\delta_{\abs x,2}$ selects exactly the weight-$2$ strings.
Across any cut the left block must announce how many $1$s it has used---$0$, $1$, or
$2$, three distinguishable messages---so the Schmidt rank, hence the bond dimension, is
$3$. (More generally $\ket{D^n_k}$ has $\chi=k+1$: the bond counts up to $k$.)

\emph{Cost.} A $\chi$-MPS is prepared by $n$ sequential unitaries on
$\lceil\log_2\chi\rceil+1$ qubits each: $2$-qubit gates for GHZ ($\chi=2$, the
$\Hgate$-plus-$\CNOT$ chain above) and $3$-qubit gates for $\ket{D^n_2}$ ($\chi=3$,
one bond register of $\lceil\log_2 3\rceil=2$ qubits coupled to each emitted qubit in
turn)---in both cases $\bigo(n)$ gates. The generic step reuses the bond register and
emits one physical qubit:
\begin{center}
\begin{quantikz}
  \lstick{bond $\ket0$}  & \gate[3]{V_k} & \qw \\
  \lstick{bond $\ket0$}  &               & \qw \\
  \lstick{fresh $\ket0$} &               & \rstick{$q_k$}\qw
\end{quantikz}
\end{center}
Applying $V_1,\dots,V_n$ in turn and finally returning the bond register to
$\ket{00}$ produces $\ket{D^n_2}$; for $\chi=2$ the bond is a single qubit and each
$V_k$ is the two-qubit gate of the chain above.
\end{remark}

We henceforth treat $O_u$ as a given oracle and count \emph{queries} to it, exactly
as we counted queries to $\ee^{-\ii Ht}$ in \cref{ch:lec05}. The state-preparation
oracle is the vector half of the input model; the rest of the lecture builds the
matrix half.

\section{Block encoding: definition and factor}
\label{sec:lec06-2}

A non-unitary $A$ cannot be realized directly by a circuit. The idea of block
encoding is to place a rescaled copy of $A$ in one \emph{block} of a larger unitary
and to access it by projecting onto that block with ancilla qubits.

\subsection*{The one-ancilla picture}

Start with the simplest case. Suppose we can find an $(n+1)$-qubit unitary $U_A$
whose top-left $n$-qubit block equals $A$,
\begin{equation}
  U_A=\begin{pmatrix} A & \ast\\ \ast & \ast\end{pmatrix},
  \label{eq:lec06-block1}
\end{equation}
where $\ast$ denotes blocks irrelevant to the computation. For any $n$-qubit state
$\ket{b}$, feed in the flagged state $\ket{0}\ket{b}$:
\begin{equation}
  U_A\ket{0}\ket{b}=\ket{0}\,A\ket{b}+\ket{1}\ket{\perp},
  \label{eq:lec06-apply1}
\end{equation}
for some (unnormalized) state $\ket{\perp}$. To see why, split every $(n+1)$-qubit
vector by its ancilla bit into a \emph{top} block (ancilla $\ket0$) and a
\emph{bottom} block (ancilla $\ket1$), each an $n$-qubit vector. (We order the ancilla
as the \emph{leading} register, so $\ket0\ket b=\ket0\otimes\ket b
=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\ket b\\ 0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ and
$\ket1\ket b=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0\\ \ket b\end{smallmatrix}\right)$: the
ancilla's $\ket0$ versus $\ket1$ amplitude selects the top versus bottom block.) In
this split the flagged input has vanishing bottom block,
$\ket{0}\ket{b}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\ket b\\ 0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$, so,
writing the lower-left block of $U_A$ as $C$,
\[
  U_A\ket{0}\ket{b}
   =\begin{pmatrix}A&\ast\\ C&\ast\end{pmatrix}\!
    \begin{pmatrix}\ket b\\ 0\end{pmatrix}
   =\begin{pmatrix}A\ket b\\ C\ket b\end{pmatrix}
   =\ket{0}\,A\ket{b}+\ket{1}\,\ket{\perp},
   \qquad \ket{\perp}\defeq C\ket{b}.
\]
Because the input's bottom block is zero, only the first block-\emph{column} of
$U_A$ is probed; of that column the top block $A$ produces the ancilla-$\ket0$ part
$\ket0\,A\ket b$, while the lower-left block $C$ dumps the leftover amplitude into the
ancilla-$\ket1$ ``junk'' state $\ket{\perp}$ (unnormalized, and irrelevant once we
post-select). Equivalently
$(\bra{0}\otimes\id)\,U_A\,(\ket{0}\otimes\id)=A$: sandwiching $U_A$ between the
ancilla flags $\ket0$ extracts exactly its top-left block. Measuring the ancilla and keeping
the outcome $0$ leaves the system in the normalized state $A\ket{b}/\norm{A\ket{b}}$,
which is obtained with success probability
\begin{equation}
  p(0)=\norm{A\ket{b}}^2=\bra{b}A\dg A\ket{b}.
  \label{eq:lec06-prob1}
\end{equation}
Thus the ``missing'' scalar $\norm{A\ket{b}}$ is recoverable from the measurement
statistics; it depends only on $A$ and $\ket{b}$, not on the irrelevant blocks of
$U_A$. Because a block of a unitary can have operator norm at most $1$, the
embedding \eqref{eq:lec06-block1} requires $\norm{A}\le1$.

\subsection*{Rescaling and the general definition}

Most matrices of interest have $\norm{A}>1$, or one simply cannot embed $A$ exactly.
Both obstacles are removed by allowing $U_A$ to encode $A/\alpha$ for a
\emph{subnormalization factor} $\alpha\ge\norm{A}$, using $a$ ancilla qubits, and
tolerating an error $\epsilon$.

\begin{definition}[Block encoding]
\label{def:lec06-be}
Let $A$ be an $n$-qubit matrix and let $\alpha,\epsilon\ge0$ and $a\in\N$. An
$(a+n)$-qubit unitary $U_A$ is an \emph{$(\alpha,a,\epsilon)$-block-encoding} of $A$
if
\begin{equation}
  \norm{\,A-\alpha\,\bigl(\bra{0^a}\otimes\id_n\bigr)\,U_A\,
         \bigl(\ket{0^a}\otimes\id_n\bigr)\,}\le\epsilon.
  \label{eq:lec06-bedef}
\end{equation}
When $\epsilon=0$ the encoding is \emph{exact} and $U_A$ is called an
$(\alpha,a)$-block-encoding. We write $U_A\in\mathrm{BE}_{\alpha,a}(A,\epsilon)$,
and $\mathrm{BE}_{\alpha,a}(A)=\mathrm{BE}_{\alpha,a}(A,0)$.
\end{definition}

Equivalently, in the exact case the top-left $n$-qubit block of $U_A$ equals
$A/\alpha$:
\begin{equation}
  \bigl(\bra{0^a}\otimes\id_n\bigr)\,U_A\,\bigl(\ket{0^a}\otimes\id_n\bigr)
  =\frac{A}{\alpha},
  \qquad
  U_A=\begin{pmatrix} A/\alpha & \ast\\ \ast & \ast\end{pmatrix},
  \label{eq:lec06-bealt}
\end{equation}
with $2^{a}$ block rows and columns. The three parameters are the currency of
Part~II:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,nosep]
  \item $\alpha$ (the \emph{subnormalization factor}) rescales $A$ into a block of a
        unitary and so obeys $\alpha\ge\norm{A}$. It is not free: applying $U_A$ to
        $\ket{0^a}\ket{b}$ produces $\ket{0^a}(A/\alpha)\ket{b}+\ket{\perp}$, so the
        flag $\ket{0^a}$ is read with probability
        $\norm{A\ket{b}}^2/\alpha^2$. A larger $\alpha$ shrinks the success
        probability and, more importantly, multiplies the query cost of every
        downstream algorithm (QSVT, QLSP) by $\alpha$. Keeping $\alpha$ close to
        $\norm{A}$ is therefore a central design goal.
  \item $a$ is the number of ancilla (flag) qubits; the useful subspace is flagged
        by $\ket{0^a}$.
  \item $\epsilon$ is the spectral-norm error, which propagates additively through
        the arithmetic of \cref{ch:lec07}.
\end{itemize}

The verification rule is worth stating separately, as it is used constantly.

\begin{remark}[Verifying a block encoding entrywise]
\label{rem:lec06-verify}
$U_A\in\mathrm{BE}_{1,a}(A)$ if and only if
$\bra{0^a,i}U_A\ket{0^a,j}=A_{ij}$ for all $i,j\in[N]$, where
$\ket{0^a,j}\defeq\ket{0^a}\ket{j}$. When $U_A=U_BU_C$ factors, one may compute the
matrix element by splitting the product,
$\bra{0^a,i}U_A\ket{0^a,j}=\bigl(U_B\dg\ket{0^a,i}\bigr)\dg\bigl(U_C\ket{0^a,j}\bigr)$,
evaluating the two states independently and taking their inner product. This is the
workhorse calculation for every construction below.
\end{remark}

\subsection*{Every contraction is block-encodable}

Block encoding is not a restrictive assumption in principle---it is the way any
contraction can be accessed---as the following construction shows.

\begin{proposition}[$(1,1)$-block-encoding is universal for contractions]
\label{prop:lec06-svd}
Every $n$-qubit matrix $A$ with $\norm{A}\le1$ admits an exact
$(1,1)$-block-encoding.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $A=W\Sigma V\dg$ be a singular value decomposition, with $W,V$ unitary and
$\Sigma=\diag(\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_N)$, $\sigma_i\in[0,1]$ since $\norm{A}\le1$.
Because $\sigma_i\le1$, the diagonal matrix $\sqrt{\id-\Sigma^2}$ is real, and the
$(n+1)$-qubit matrix
\begin{equation}
  U_A=\begin{pmatrix} W & 0\\ 0 & \id_n\end{pmatrix}
      \begin{pmatrix} \Sigma & \sqrt{\id_n-\Sigma^2}\\
                      \sqrt{\id_n-\Sigma^2} & -\Sigma\end{pmatrix}
      \begin{pmatrix} V\dg & 0\\ 0 & \id_n\end{pmatrix}
  \label{eq:lec06-svd}
\end{equation}
is unitary: the outer factors are unitary, and the middle factor is unitary because
each $2\times2$ block $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\sigma_i&\sqrt{1-\sigma_i^2}\\
\sqrt{1-\sigma_i^2}&-\sigma_i\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ is a reflection (its columns
are orthonormal). Its top-left block is $W\Sigma V\dg=A$, so
$U_A\in\mathrm{BE}_{1,1}(A)$.
\end{proof}

\cref{prop:lec06-svd} says block encoding costs nothing in principle. The content of
Part~II is that for \emph{structured} $A$---sparse matrices (\cref{sec:lec06-3}),
sums and products of block encodings (\cref{ch:lec07}), and matrices arising from
physical Hamiltonians---one can build $U_A$ with a $\polylog(N)$-size circuit and a
small factor $\alpha$, so that the whole pipeline stays efficient.

\begin{example}[Block-encoding a $2\times2$ matrix; reading off $\alpha$]
\label{exa:lec06-2x2}
Consider the single-qubit matrix
\[
  A=\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1\\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Its singular values $\sigma_\pm$ satisfy $\sigma_+\sigma_-=\abs{\det A}=1$ and
$\sigma_+^2+\sigma_-^2=\tr(A^\top A)=3$, so $\sigma_\pm^2=(3\pm\sqrt5)/2$ and
$\sigma_\pm=\varphi^{\pm1}$ with $\varphi=\tfrac{1+\sqrt5}{2}\approx1.618$ the golden
ratio; hence $\norm{A}=\sigma_+=\varphi$. Since $\norm{A}>1$, no exact
$(1,\cdot)$-block-encoding exists; take $\alpha=2\ge\norm{A}$ and
\[
  M\defeq\frac{A}{\alpha}=\begin{pmatrix}1/2&1/2\\0&1/2\end{pmatrix},
  \qquad \norm{M}=\frac{\varphi}{2}\approx0.809<1 ,
\]
a contraction. The dilation of \cref{prop:lec06-svd} completes $M$ to a unitary by
appending the defect blocks $\sqrt{\id-MM^\top}$ and $\sqrt{\id-M^\top M}$:
\[
  U_A=\begin{pmatrix} M & \sqrt{\id-MM^\top}\\[3pt]
                      \sqrt{\id-M^\top M} & -M^\top\end{pmatrix}
     \approx\begin{pmatrix}
        0.5 & 0.5 & 0.688 & -0.162\\
        0   & 0.5 & -0.162 & 0.851\\
        0.851 & -0.162 & -0.5 & 0\\
        -0.162 & 0.688 & -0.5 & -0.5
      \end{pmatrix},
\]
a genuine $2$-qubit orthogonal matrix (one flag qubit, one system qubit) whose
top-left $2\times2$ block is exactly $M=A/\alpha$; this is a $(2,1)$-block-encoding of
$A$. The defect blocks are the ``starred'' entries of the schematic form above, fixed
(up to the sign convention of the dilation) by demanding orthonormal columns.

Applying $U_A$ to $\ket{0}\ket{b}$ and post-selecting the flag $\ket{0}$ returns
$A\ket{b}/\norm{A\ket{b}}$ with probability $\norm{A\ket{b}}^2/\alpha^2$. Explicitly,
for $\ket{b}=\ket{0}$ we have $A\ket{0}=\ket{0}$, so $\norm{A\ket{0}}^2=1$ and the
success probability is $1/\alpha^2=1/4$; for $\ket{b}=\ket{1}$ we have
$A\ket{1}=\ket{0}+\ket{1}$, so $\norm{A\ket{1}}^2=2$ and the probability is
$2/4=1/2$. Had we been wasteful and chosen $\alpha=10$, the identical output states
would still emerge, but these probabilities would fall to $1/100$ and $1/50$---smaller
by the factor $(\alpha/2)^2=25$, directly lengthening the runtime. This is the
practical meaning of ``keep $\alpha$ close to $\norm{A}$.''
\end{example}

\section{Matrix--vector multiplication: the first primitive}
\label{sec:lec06-matvec}

With both input models in hand we can already perform the most basic
quantum numerical-linear-algebra operation: given a matrix $A$ as a block encoding
and a vector $b$ as a state-preparation oracle, produce the normalized
matrix--vector product $A\ket{b}/\norm{A\ket{b}}$. This one primitive is the engine
inside linear solvers, power iteration, and the read-out of a simulated state
$\ee^{-\ii Ht}\ket{b}$.

\begin{proposition}[Applying a block encoding to a state]
\label{prop:lec06-matvec}
Let $U_A\in\mathrm{BE}_{\alpha,a}(A)$ and let $O_b$ prepare $\ket{b}=O_b\ket{0^n}$.
Then
\[
  U_A\,\bigl(\id_a\otimes O_b\bigr)\ket{0^a}\ket{0^n}
  =\ket{0^a}\,\frac{A\ket{b}}{\alpha}+\ket{\Phi^{\perp}},
  \qquad \bigl(\proj{0^a}\otimes\id\bigr)\ket{\Phi^{\perp}}=0,
\]
and measuring the $a$-qubit flag returns $\ket{0^a}$ with probability
$p=\norm{A\ket{b}}^2/\alpha^2$, upon which the system register collapses to
$A\ket{b}/\norm{A\ket{b}}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$O_b$ produces $\ket{0^a}\ket{b}$; applying $U_A$ and using
$(\bra{0^a}\otimes\id)U_A(\ket{0^a}\otimes\id)=A/\alpha$ (\cref{def:lec06-be}) gives
the stated split, whose flag-$\ket{0^a}$ part is $\ket{0^a}(A/\alpha)\ket{b}$. Its
squared norm $\norm{(A/\alpha)\ket{b}}^2=\norm{A\ket{b}}^2/\alpha^2$ is the
probability of reading $\ket{0^a}$, and the post-measurement system state is the
normalized $A\ket{b}/\norm{A\ket{b}}$.
\end{proof}

Three features make this the template for all of Part~II.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,nosep]
  \item \emph{Input and output share one format.} The result is again an $n$-qubit
        state, like the input $\ket{b}$. Block encoding is thus both an \emph{input}
        and an \emph{output} model: the product feeds straight into another block
        encoding, so matrix--vector products chain into $A_k\cdots A_1\ket{b}$ with no
        read-out of intermediate vectors.
  \item \emph{Cost is set by $\alpha$ and the output norm.} One run succeeds with
        probability $p=\norm{A\ket{b}}^2/\alpha^2$, so naive repetition costs
        $\bigo(\alpha^2/\norm{A\ket{b}}^2)$ queries. Amplitude amplification
        (\cref{thm:lec03-aa}) removes the square and brings this to
        $\bigo\!\bigl(\alpha/\norm{A\ket{b}}\bigr)$ queries to $U_A$ and $O_b$---the
        quadratic gain that makes the primitive worthwhile, and the quantitative
        reason to keep $\alpha$ small: every matrix--vector product pays for it
        linearly.
  \item \emph{Only the direction is produced.} The scalar $\norm{A\ket{b}}$ is not
        carried by the output state; it is recovered from the success probability as
        $\norm{A\ket{b}}=\alpha\sqrt p$, exactly as the norm of a prepared vector had
        to be tracked classically in \cref{sec:lec06-1}.
\end{itemize}

\begin{example}[Matrix--vector product with the $2\times2$ encoding]
\label{exa:lec06-matvec}
Take $A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ with the
$(2,1)$-block-encoding of \cref{exa:lec06-2x2} ($\alpha=2$). For $\ket{b}=\ket{0}$
(so $O_b=\id$), $A\ket{0}=\ket{0}$ with $\norm{A\ket{0}}=1$, so the flag is read with
probability $p=1/\alpha^2=1/4$ and the output is $\ket{0}$. For $\ket{b}=\ket{1}$,
$A\ket{1}=\ket{0}+\ket{1}$ has $\norm{A\ket{1}}=\sqrt2$, giving $p=2/4=1/2$ and output
$(\ket{0}+\ket{1})/\sqrt2$. The larger the output norm, the larger the success
probability; and had we used the wasteful $\alpha=10$ of \cref{exa:lec06-2x2}, both
probabilities would drop by a factor $25$.
\end{example}

Block encodings and state-preparation oracles also \emph{combine} to make new ones:
one can add two block-encoded matrices, add two prepared vectors, and multiply two
block-encoded matrices, so that the output is again a block encoding (or a state)
ready for the next step. Each such rule is a special case of the \emph{linear
combination of unitaries} (LCU), so we develop them all together---for an arbitrary
number of terms, with their circuits, error propagation, and $\ell^1$-norm
cost---in \cref{ch:lec07}.

\section{Block encoding of \texorpdfstring{$s$}{s}-sparse matrices}
\label{sec:lec06-3}

We now give the canonical efficient construction: a block encoding of a sparse
matrix built from two oracles---one that supplies entry \emph{values}, one that
supplies nonzero \emph{locations}. This is the model under which almost all QNLA
complexity is stated.

\subsection*{The entry and location oracles}

Assume $\norm{A}_{\max}\defeq\max_{ij}\abs{A_{ij}}\le1$ (rescale $A$ by
$\norm{A}_{\max}$ otherwise). The \emph{entry oracle} encodes each matrix element in
the amplitude of a signal qubit by a controlled rotation:
\begin{equation}
  O_A\ket{0}\ket{i}\ket{j}
   =\Bigl(A_{ij}\ket{0}+\sqrt{1-\abs{A_{ij}}^2}\,\ket{1}\Bigr)\ket{i}\ket{j}.
  \label{eq:lec06-OA}
\end{equation}
(In practice a database oracle
$\widetilde O_A\ket{0^{d}}\ket{i}\ket{j}=\ket{\tilde A_{ij}}\ket{i}\ket{j}$ returning
a $d$-bit representation of $A_{ij}$ is converted into \eqref{eq:lec06-OA} by a
controlled rotation followed by uncomputation of the work register.) A matrix is
\emph{$s$-sparse} if every row and column has at most $s$ nonzero entries; the
\emph{location oracle} lists them. For a $1$-sparse matrix each column $j$ has a
single nonzero entry in row $c(j)$, and $c$ is a permutation, so there is a unitary
\begin{equation}
  O_c\ket{j}=\ket{c(j)},\qquad O_c\dg\ket{c(j)}=\ket{j}.
  \label{eq:lec06-Oc1}
\end{equation}
For general $s$-sparse $A$, write $c(j,\ell)$ for the row of the $\ell$-th nonzero
entry in column $j$ ($\ell\in[s]$, $s=2^{s'}$), and assume a unitary
\begin{equation}
  O_c\ket{\ell}\ket{j}=\ket{\ell}\ket{c(j,\ell)}.
  \label{eq:lec06-Ocs}
\end{equation}
Reversibility of $O_c$ requires that, given the row $i=c(j,\ell)$ and the index
$\ell$, the column $j$ be recoverable; a banded matrix with
$c(j,\ell)=j+\ell-\ell_0\pmod N$ satisfies this. The entry oracle carries the extra
$\ell$-register along,
\begin{equation}
  O_A\ket{0}\ket{\ell}\ket{j}
   =\Bigl(A_{c(j,\ell),j}\ket{0}
     +\sqrt{1-\abs{A_{c(j,\ell),j}}^2}\,\ket{1}\Bigr)\ket{\ell}\ket{j}.
  \label{eq:lec06-OAs}
\end{equation}

\subsection*{The one-sparse case}

\begin{proposition}[Block encoding of a $1$-sparse matrix]
\label{prop:lec06-1sparse}
Let $A$ be $1$-sparse with $\norm{A}_{\max}\le1$, accessed via $O_c$ of
\eqref{eq:lec06-Oc1} and $O_A$ of \eqref{eq:lec06-OA}. Then
$U_A\defeq(\id\otimes O_c)\,O_A$ is a $(1,1)$-block-encoding of $A$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Apply $U_A$ to the flagged basis state $\ket{0}\ket{j}$ and take the overlap with
$\ket{0}\ket{i}$:
\[
  \bra{0}\bra{i}\,U_A\,\ket{0}\ket{j}
  =\bra{0}\bra{i}\Bigl(A_{c(j),j}\ket{0}
     +\sqrt{1-\abs{A_{c(j),j}}^2}\,\ket{1}\Bigr)\ket{c(j)}
  =A_{c(j),j}\,\delta_{i,c(j)}.
\]
The $\ket{1}$ branch is annihilated by $\bra{0}$, and $\delta_{i,c(j)}$ selects the
unique nonzero row, so the overlap equals $A_{ij}$ (which is $A_{c(j),j}$ when
$i=c(j)$ and $0$ otherwise). By \cref{rem:lec06-verify},
$U_A\in\mathrm{BE}_{1,1}(A)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[A $1$-sparse worked encoding: the cyclic shift]
\label{exa:lec06-shift}
Let $N=4$ and let $A=S$ be the cyclic shift $S\ket{j}=\ket{(j+1)\bmod4}$, a
permutation matrix; it is $1$-sparse with all nonzero entries equal to $1$, so
$c(j)=(j+1)\bmod4$ and $A_{c(j),j}=1$. The entry oracle \eqref{eq:lec06-OA} is
trivial here, $O_A\ket{0}\ket{i}\ket{j}=\ket{0}\ket{i}\ket{j}$ (all magnitudes are
$1$, so no rotation onto $\ket{1}$), and $O_c\ket{j}=\ket{(j+1)\bmod4}=S\ket{j}$.
Then $U_A=(\id\otimes O_c)O_A$ acts as $\ket{0}\ket{j}\mapsto\ket{0}\ket{(j+1)
\bmod4}$, and $\bra{0,i}U_A\ket{0,j}=\delta_{i,(j+1)\bmod4}=S_{ij}$. Since $S$ is
already unitary, $U_A$ block-encodes it with $\alpha=1$ and the flag qubit is
inert---a reassuring sanity check that a unitary is its own $(1,0)$-block-encoding
(here padded to one idle ancilla).
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Block encoding of a diagonal matrix]
\label{exa:lec06-diag}
For a diagonal $A=\diag(A_{00},\dots,A_{N-1,N-1})$ the location map is the identity,
$c(j)=j$, so $O_c=\id$ and the entry oracle collapses to
$O_A\ket{0}\ket{i}=(A_{ii}\ket{0}+\sqrt{1-\abs{A_{ii}}^2}\ket{1})\ket{i}$. Taking
$U_A=O_A$ gives $\bra{0,i}U_A\ket{0,j}=A_{ii}\delta_{ij}$, so
$U_A\in\mathrm{BE}_{1,1}(A)$: a diagonal matrix is block-encoded by a single
multiply-controlled rotation.
\end{example}

\subsection*{The general $s$-sparse case}

For $s>1$ we superpose over the $s$ nonzero locations in each column. Let
$D\defeq\Hgate^{\otimes s'}$ be the \emph{diffusion} operator (the term is inherited
from Grover's search, \cref{ch:lec03}), acting on the $s'$-qubit $\ell$-register as
\begin{equation}
  D\ket{0^{s'}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt s}\sum_{\ell\in[s]}\ket{\ell}.
  \label{eq:lec06-diff}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Block encoding of an $s$-sparse matrix]
\label{thm:lec06-ssparse}
Let $A$ be $s$-sparse with $\norm{A}_{\max}\le1$, accessed via the location oracle
$O_c$ of \eqref{eq:lec06-Ocs} and the entry oracle $O_A$ of \eqref{eq:lec06-OAs}.
Define
\begin{equation}
  U_A\defeq\bigl(\id\otimes D\otimes\id\bigr)\,
           \bigl(\id\otimes O_c\bigr)\,O_A\,
           \bigl(\id\otimes D\otimes\id\bigr),
  \label{eq:lec06-Uss}
\end{equation}
where the two $D$'s act on the $\ell$-register and the middle $O_c$ writes the row
index. Then $U_A\in\mathrm{BE}_{s,\,s'+1}(A)$: it is an $(s,s'+1)$-block-encoding of
$A$ with subnormalization factor $\alpha=s$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The ancilla registers are the signal qubit (call it $\ket{0}$) and the $s'$-qubit
$\ell$-register; the flag is $\ket{0}\ket{0^{s'}}$. Call $\ket{0}\ket{0^{s'}}\ket{j}$
the \emph{source} and $\ket{0}\ket{0^{s'}}\ket{i}$ the \emph{target}. Propagate the
source through $D$, then $O_A$, then $O_c$:
\begin{align*}
  \ket{0}\ket{0^{s'}}\ket{j}
  &\xrightarrow{\ D\ }
    \frac{1}{\sqrt s}\sum_{\ell\in[s]}\ket{0}\ket{\ell}\ket{j}\\
  &\xrightarrow{\ O_A\ }
    \frac{1}{\sqrt s}\sum_{\ell\in[s]}
    \Bigl(A_{c(j,\ell),j}\ket{0}+\sqrt{1-\abs{A_{c(j,\ell),j}}^2}\ket{1}\Bigr)
    \ket{\ell}\ket{j}\\
  &\xrightarrow{\ O_c\ }
    \frac{1}{\sqrt s}\sum_{\ell\in[s]}
    \Bigl(A_{c(j,\ell),j}\ket{0}+\sqrt{1-\abs{A_{c(j,\ell),j}}^2}\ket{1}\Bigr)
    \ket{\ell}\ket{c(j,\ell)}.
\end{align*}
Since only the flag component survives, evolve the target backward, equivalently
apply the (Hermitian) $D$ to it:
\[
  \ket{0}\ket{0^{s'}}\ket{i}\xrightarrow{\ D\ }
   \frac{1}{\sqrt s}\sum_{\ell'\in[s]}\ket{0}\ket{\ell'}\ket{i}.
\]
The block matrix element is the inner product of the two, in which $\bra{0}$ kills
the $\ket{1}$ branch and $\braket{\ell'|\ell}=\delta_{\ell\ell'}$,
$\braket{i|c(j,\ell)}=\delta_{i,c(j,\ell)}$ collapse the double sum:
\[
  \bra{0}\bra{0^{s'}}\bra{i}\,U_A\,\ket{0}\ket{0^{s'}}\ket{j}
  =\frac{1}{s}\sum_{\ell}A_{c(j,\ell),j}\,\delta_{i,c(j,\ell)}
  =\frac{1}{s}A_{ij},
\]
because for each row $i$ there is a unique $\ell$ with $c(j,\ell)=i$ (or the term is
absent, giving $A_{ij}=0$). Thus the top-left block is $A/s$, i.e.\
$U_A\in\mathrm{BE}_{s,s'+1}(A)$.
\end{proof}

The factor $\alpha=s$ is exactly the price of the two diffusion operators: each $D$
spreads a unit amplitude over $s$ locations with weight $1/\sqrt s$, and the two of
them contribute $1/s$ to the surviving block. This is why sparsity controls cost in
QNLA: an $s$-sparse matrix is block-encoded with $\alpha=s$, and downstream query
counts scale linearly in $\alpha$.

\begin{remark}[More general location oracles]
\label{rem:lec06-genloc}
The single-oracle model \eqref{eq:lec06-Ocs} is restrictive: it demands that the row
$c(j,\ell)$ be a fixed function of $(j,\ell)$ with a matching inverse, which is
natural only for banded matrices. A more flexible model uses two oracles,
$O_c\ket{\ell}\ket{j}=\ket{c(j,\ell)}\ket{j}$ and
$O_r\ket{\ell}\ket{i}=\ket{r(i,\ell)}\ket{i}$, listing the $\ell$-th nonzero in
column $j$ and row $i$ respectively; inserting a \textsc{swap} between them yields
$U_A\in\mathrm{BE}_{s,n+1}(A)$ by the same inner-product bookkeeping (this is the
construction of Gily\'en--Su--Low--Wiebe, up to two saved qubits). The value oracle
is correspondingly controlled on both indices,
$O_A\ket{0}\ket{i}\ket{j}=(A_{ij}\ket{0}+\sqrt{1-\abs{A_{ij}}^2}\ket{1})\ket{i}
\ket{j}$; entries with $A_{ij}=0$ never contribute, so the rotation there is
immaterial.
\end{remark}

\subsection*{Two worked encodings: the Laplacian and the Ising model}

The $s$-sparse recipe \eqref{eq:lec06-Uss} turns two physically central operators into
block encodings with explicit circuits. Both are Hermitian and banded in a product
basis, so the location oracle is a shift (or a bit flip) and the entry oracle loads a
handful of constants. In each the circuit is the sandwich
$U_A=(\id\otimes D)(\id\otimes O_c)\,O_A\,(\id\otimes D)$ of
\cref{thm:lec06-ssparse}, with $D=\Hgate^{\otimes s'}$ spreading an
$s'=\lceil\log_2 s\rceil$-qubit index register.

\begin{example}[Finite-difference Laplacian in $\R^d$]
\label{exa:lec06-laplacian}
Discretize $-\Delta$ on a periodic $d$-dimensional grid with $N=2^m$ points per axis,
so the position is $\ket{\mathbf x}=\ket{x_1}\cdots\ket{x_d}$ on $dm$ qubits. With the
per-axis cyclic shift $S_k\colon x_k\mapsto x_k{+}1\bmod N$, the negative Laplacian is
the sum of second differences
\[
  L=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\bigl(2\id-S_k-S_k\dg\bigr),
\]
a real symmetric matrix that is $(2d{+}1)$-sparse: each grid point $\mathbf x$ couples
to itself (diagonal entry $2d$) and to its $2d$ neighbors $\mathbf x\pm\mathbf e_k$
(entry $-1$). Hence $\norm{L}_{\max}=2d$; set $\hat L\defeq L/(2d)$, with diagonal $1$
and off-diagonals $-\tfrac1{2d}$. Index the $s=2d{+}1$ nonzeros by
$\ell\in\{0\}\cup\{(k,\pm)\}$. The \emph{location oracle} is the $\ell$-controlled
shift
\[
  O_c\ket{\ell}\ket{\mathbf x}=\ket{\ell}\ket{\mathbf x+\delta_\ell},\qquad
  \delta_0=\mathbf 0,\quad \delta_{(k,\pm)}=\pm\mathbf e_k,
\]
a $\pm1$ increment of the \emph{selected} coordinate register---the modular adder of
\cref{sec:lec04-qftadder}. The \emph{entry oracle} is independent of $\mathbf x$: it
keeps the signal in $\ket0$ for $\ell=0$ and rotates it to carry amplitude $-\tfrac1{2d}$
otherwise,
\[
  O_{\hat L}\ket0\ket\ell=
   \begin{cases}\ket0\ket\ell, & \ell=0,\\[2pt]
   \bigl(-\tfrac1{2d}\ket0+\sqrt{1-\tfrac1{4d^2}}\,\ket1\bigr)\ket\ell, & \ell\neq0.
   \end{cases}
\]
\cref{thm:lec06-ssparse} then gives $U_{\hat L}\in\mathrm{BE}_{2d+1,\,s'+1}(\hat L)$:
\begin{center}
\begin{quantikz}
  \lstick{$\ket0$ \,\scriptsize(signal)}        & \qw          & \gate[2]{O_{\hat L}} & \qw           & \qw \\
  \lstick{$\ket{0^{s'}}$ \,\scriptsize($\ell$)} & \gate{D}     &                      & \gate[2]{O_c} & \gate{D} \\
  \lstick{$\ket{\mathbf x}$}                    & \qwbundle{dm} & \qw                  &               & \qw
\end{quantikz}
\end{center}
In one dimension ($d=1$, $s=3$) the index is two qubits, $O_c$ is ``increment $x$ if
$\ell=1$, decrement if $\ell=2$, idle if $\ell=0$,'' and $O_{\hat L}$ is one controlled
$R_y\!\bigl(2\arccos\tfrac12\bigr)=R_y(2\pi/3)$ (with a sign) on the two off-diagonal
branches. The cost is $2d$ controlled modular adders and $\bigo(1)$ rotations, and the
subnormalization is $\alpha=2d{+}1$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Transverse-field Ising model]
\label{exa:lec06-ising}
The $n$-spin TFIM $H=-J\sum_i Z_iZ_{i+1}-h\sum_i X_i$ is $(n{+}1)$-sparse in the
computational basis:
\[
  H\ket{\mathbf x}=E(\mathbf x)\,\ket{\mathbf x}-h\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ket{\mathbf x\oplus\mathbf e_i},
  \qquad E(\mathbf x)=-J\sum_i(-1)^{x_i\oplus x_{i+1}},
\]
one diagonal entry plus $n$ single-spin-flip neighbors of amplitude $-h$. So
$\norm{H}_{\max}=\max\{(n{-}1)J,\,h\}$; write $\hat H=H/\norm{H}_{\max}$. Index the
nonzeros by $\ell\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}$. The \emph{location oracle} is the
$\ell$-multiplexed bit flip
\[
  O_c\ket{\ell}\ket{\mathbf x}=\ket{\ell}\ket{\mathbf x\oplus\mathbf e_\ell}\ (\ell\ge1),
  \qquad O_c\ket{0}\ket{\mathbf x}=\ket{0}\ket{\mathbf x},
\]
a \textsc{select} of single-qubit $X$ gates. The \emph{entry oracle} loads the
diagonal energy $E(\mathbf x)/\norm{H}_{\max}$ on the $\ell=0$ branch (a rotation whose
angle is computed from the nearest-neighbor parities $x_i\oplus x_{i+1}$---say by
$n{-}1$ \textsc{cnot}s accumulating the parity count) and the constant
$-h/\norm{H}_{\max}$ on each $\ell\ge1$ branch. \cref{thm:lec06-ssparse} gives
$U_{\hat H}\in\mathrm{BE}_{n+1,\,s'+1}(\hat H)$ with $s'=\lceil\log_2(n{+}1)\rceil$:
\begin{center}
\begin{quantikz}
  \lstick{$\ket0$ \,\scriptsize(signal)}        & \qw         & \gate[3]{O_{\hat H}} & \qw           & \qw \\
  \lstick{$\ket{0^{s'}}$ \,\scriptsize($\ell$)} & \gate{D}    &                      & \gate[2]{O_c} & \gate{D} \\
  \lstick{$\ket{\mathbf x}$ \,\scriptsize($n$ spins)} & \qwbundle{n} &                 &               & \qw
\end{quantikz}
\end{center}
Here $O_{\hat H}$ reads the spin register (for the $\ell=0$ diagonal), whereas
$O_c$ only flips the spin addressed by $\ell$. Because $H$ is \emph{also} a sum of just
$2n{-}1$ Pauli strings, the LCU route of \cref{ch:lec07} block-encodes it with
subnormalization $\norm{c}_1=(n{-}1)J+nh$ rather than the sparsity bound
$\alpha=n{+}1$, and avoids the $\mathbf x$-dependent entry rotation; see
\cref{exa:lec07-tfim}. Which is cheaper depends on $J,h$, but for local Hamiltonians
the LCU encoding is usually preferred.
\end{example}

\section{Hermitian block encoding}
\label{sec:lec06-4}

The block encoding of \cref{def:lec06-be} constrains only the top-left block of
$U_A$; the unitary itself need not be Hermitian even when $A$ is. Yet several
downstream constructions---most importantly qubitization (\cref{ch:lec08})---are
cleanest when the encoding unitary is \emph{itself} Hermitian.

\begin{definition}[Hermitian block encoding]
\label{def:lec06-hbe}
An $(\alpha,a,\epsilon)$-block-encoding $U_A$ of $A$ is a
\emph{$(\alpha,a,\epsilon)$-Hermitian-block-encoding} if $U_A=U_A\dg$. We write
$U_A\in\mathrm{HBE}_{\alpha,a}(A,\epsilon)$, with
$\mathrm{HBE}_{\alpha,a}(A)=\mathrm{HBE}_{\alpha,a}(A,0)$.
\end{definition}

Two cautions frame the definition. First, $A$ being Hermitian does \emph{not} make a
given block encoding Hermitian: the $1$-sparse encoding $U_A=(\id\otimes O_c)O_A$ of
\cref{prop:lec06-1sparse} applied to a Hermitian diagonal $A$ is generally not
self-adjoint (\cref{ex:lec06-4}). Second, a Hermitian $U_A$ that is also unitary
squares to the identity, $U_A^2=\id$, so $U_A$ is a \emph{reflection}; its spectrum
is $\{\pm1\}$. This is precisely the structure qubitization exploits: a Hermitian
block encoding of a Hermitian $A$ makes the pair
$(U_A,\;2\proj{0^a}\otimes\id-\id)$ generate an $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ on each eigenspace of
$A$, turning matrix functions of $A$ into single-qubit rotations. We defer that
machinery to \cref{ch:lec08} and here only record how to \emph{build} a Hermitian
encoding for a Hermitian sparse matrix.

\begin{theorem}[Hermitian block encoding of a Hermitian $s$-sparse matrix]
\label{thm:lec06-hbe}
Let $A$ be Hermitian and $s$-sparse with $\norm{A}_{\max}\le1$. Suppose the
locations are accessed through a single oracle
$O_c\ket{\ell}\ket{j}=\ket{c(j,\ell)}\ket{j}$ (using Hermiticity, the $\ell$-th
nonzero of column $j$ is also the $\ell$-th nonzero of row $j$), and the entries
through a square-root value oracle
\begin{equation}
  O_A\ket{i}\ket{0}\ket{j}
   =\ket{i}\Bigl(\sqrt{A_{ij}}\,\ket{0}+\sqrt{1-\abs{A_{ij}}}\,\ket{1}\Bigr)\ket{j},
  \label{eq:lec06-OAsqrt}
\end{equation}
where for complex $A_{ij}=\abs{A_{ij}}\ee^{\ii\theta_{ij}}$ the branch
$\sqrt{A_{ij}}=\sqrt{\abs{A_{ij}}}\,\ee^{\ii\theta_{ij}/2}$ is chosen. Then there is
a circuit built from $D$, $O_c$, $O_A$, a \textsc{swap}, and their inverses that
realizes $U_A\in\mathrm{HBE}_{s,\,n+2}(A)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof idea]
Use \emph{two} signal qubits and two index registers, with computational states
$\ket{a}\ket{i}\ket{b}\ket{j}$, $a,b\in\{0,1\}$, and let \textsc{swap} exchange the
two $(a,i)\leftrightarrow(b,j)$ halves. Propagating the source
$\ket{0}\ket{0^n}\ket{0}\ket{j}$ through $D,O_c,O_A$ deposits a factor
$\sqrt{A_{c(j,\ell),j}}$ on the surviving branch; the \textsc{swap} then makes the
circuit symmetric under $i\leftrightarrow j$, and propagating the target
$\ket{0}\ket{0^n}\ket{0}\ket{i}$ through the mirror-image gates deposits a matching
$\sqrt{A_{c(i,\ell'),i}}$. Taking the inner product and using Hermiticity
$A_{ij}=A_{ji}^{\ast}$,
\[
  \bra{0}\bra{0^n}\bra{0}\bra{i}\,U_A\,\ket{0}\ket{0^n}\ket{0}\ket{j}
  =\frac{1}{s}\sqrt{A_{ij}}\sqrt{A_{ji}^{\ast}}
   \sum_{\ell,\ell'}\delta_{i,c(j,\ell)}\delta_{j,c(i,\ell')}
  =\frac{1}{s}A_{ij},
\]
since $\sqrt{A_{ij}}\sqrt{A_{ji}^{\ast}}=\sqrt{A_{ij}}\sqrt{A_{ij}}=A_{ij}$ and each
Kronecker delta is satisfied by a unique index. The symmetric placement of $O_A$ and
$O_A\dg$ about the \textsc{swap} makes the whole circuit equal to its own adjoint, so
$U_A$ is Hermitian; the factor $1/s$ from the two diffusion operators gives
$\alpha=s$, and the register count is $n+2$. This is the Childs--Kothari--Somma
quantum-walk construction.
\end{proof}

\section{Block encoding beyond the computational basis}
\label{sec:lec06-5}

So far the useful block has been flagged by the \emph{computational} state
$\ket{0^a}$ on the ancilla register. Nothing in the algebra requires this: the flag
can be any subspace, singled out by a projector. This generalization is what lets
block encodings compose (\cref{ch:lec07}) and underlies qubitization
(\cref{ch:lec08}).

\begin{definition}[Projected (generalized) block encoding]
\label{def:lec06-proj}
Let $U$ be a unitary on a space $\Hilb'\supseteq\Hilb$, and let $\Pi,\Pi'$ be
orthogonal projectors on $\Hilb'$ with images isomorphic to $\Hilb$ via fixed
isometries. Then $U$ is an \emph{$(\alpha,\epsilon)$-projected-block-encoding} of an
operator $A$ on $\Hilb$ if
\begin{equation}
  \norm{\,A-\alpha\,\Pi'\,U\,\Pi\,}\le\epsilon,
  \label{eq:lec06-projdef}
\end{equation}
where the norm is taken after identifying $\operatorname{im}\Pi$ and
$\operatorname{im}\Pi'$ with $\Hilb$.
\end{definition}

The computational-basis \cref{def:lec06-be} is the special case
$\Pi=\Pi'=\proj{0^a}\otimes\id_n$ and $\Pi'U\Pi=(\ket{0^a}\bra{0^a}\otimes\id)U
(\ket{0^a}\bra{0^a}\otimes\id)$, whose nontrivial block is exactly
$(\bra{0^a}\otimes\id)U(\ket{0^a}\otimes\id)$. Allowing $\Pi\neq\Pi'$ accommodates
rectangular $A$ and asymmetric encodings; allowing a general $\Pi$ lets the flag be
prepared by \emph{any} state-preparation unitary rather than by the bare $\ket{0^a}$.

\begin{example}[LCU flag: a linear combination of unitaries]
\label{exa:lec06-lcu}
Anticipating \cref{ch:lec07}, suppose $A=\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}\beta_k V_k$ with
$\beta_k>0$ and each $V_k$ unitary, and set $\alpha=\sum_k\beta_k$. Let $\mathrm{PREP}$
be the state-preparation unitary (\cref{def:lec06-sp}) with
$\mathrm{PREP}\ket{0^a}=\frac{1}{\sqrt\alpha}\sum_k\sqrt{\beta_k}\,\ket{k}$, and let
$\mathrm{SEL}=\sum_k\proj{k}\otimes V_k$ be the multiplexed ``select'' unitary. Then
$U=(\mathrm{PREP}\dg\otimes\id)\,\mathrm{SEL}\,(\mathrm{PREP}\otimes\id)$ is an
$(\alpha,a)$-block-encoding of $A$, because
\[
  \bigl(\bra{0^a}\otimes\id\bigr)U\bigl(\ket{0^a}\otimes\id\bigr)
  =\sum_k\bigl(\tfrac{\sqrt{\beta_k}}{\sqrt\alpha}\bigr)^{\!2}\alpha\cdot
   \frac{V_k}{1}
  =\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_k\beta_k V_k=\frac{A}{\alpha}.
\]
Here the flag subspace $\operatorname{im}\Pi$ is $\ket{0^a}$ \emph{as seen through}
$\mathrm{PREP}$; the ``beyond the computational basis'' viewpoint of
\cref{def:lec06-proj} makes this manifest, since $\Pi=\mathrm{PREP}\proj{0^a}
\mathrm{PREP}\dg$ projects onto the prepared coefficient state. This LCU block
encoding, with subnormalization $\alpha=\sum_k\beta_k$ the $\ell_1$-norm of the
coefficients, is the second pillar of QNLA and the subject of the next lecture.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Why the projector form is the right abstraction]
\label{rem:lec06-abstraction}
The generalized form \eqref{eq:lec06-projdef} exposes what block encoding really
is: a unitary $U$ together with a way of ``entering'' ($\Pi$) and ``exiting''
($\Pi'$) a distinguished subspace on which $U$ acts as $A/\alpha$. Products of block
encodings multiply the operators (with the projectors composing), sums combine
through an LCU flag, and---crucially for \cref{ch:lec08,ch:lec09}---the singular
values of $A$ become the parameters of the $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ rotations that
$U,\Pi,\Pi'$ generate on the two-dimensional invariant subspaces. Every algorithm in
Part~II is, at bottom, a statement about polynomials of the operator $\Pi'U\Pi$.
\end{remark}

\section{Quantum advantage: where it lives, and three caveats}
\label{sec:lec06-advantage}

It is worth stating plainly where the speedup in these operations comes from---and
where it can silently evaporate. An $n$-qubit register stores $N=2^n$ complex
amplitudes, and a \emph{structured} block encoding together with a state-preparation
oracle applies $A/\alpha$ to $\ket{b}$ with a circuit of only $\polylog(N)$ gates
(\cref{sec:lec06-3} does this for sparse $A$). Classically, multiplying a vector by a
dense $N\times N$ matrix costs $\bigo(N^2)$, and by an $s$-sparse one $\bigo(sN)$---
both at least linear in the exponentially large dimension. So matrix--vector
multiplication, and likewise the sums and products of \cref{ch:lec07}, act on
$2^n$-dimensional objects in time $\polylog(N)$: an \emph{exponential advantage in the
dimension}. Three caveats decide whether that advantage is real.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,nosep]
  \item \emph{Input.} The speedup assumes the inputs are cheap to load. A generic
        vector needs $\Omega(2^n/n)$ gates to prepare (\cref{prop:lec06-sp}) and a
        generic matrix has no $\polylog$ block encoding; only structured
        data---sparse, efficiently integrable, or stored in QRAM---qualifies. If
        loading the data already costs $\bigo(N)$, there is no advantage.
  \item \emph{Output.} The result $A\ket{b}/\norm{A\ket{b}}$ is a quantum state of
        $N$ amplitudes. Reading all of them out requires $\Omega(N)$ measurements,
        which again erases the gain. The advantage survives only when the sought
        answer is a \emph{succinct summary} of the output---an expectation value
        $\bra{\psi}M\ket{\psi}$, an overlap, a sample, a single bit---rather than the
        whole vector.
  \item \emph{Normalization.} The subnormalization $\alpha$ (and, for the arithmetic
        of \cref{ch:lec07}, the $\ell^1$ factor $\norm{c}_1$ and the product
        $\prod_j\alpha_j$) enters the success probability: a matrix--vector product
        needs $\bigo(\alpha/\norm{A\ket{b}})$ repetitions
        (\cref{sec:lec06-matvec}). The speedup is thus exponential in the dimension
        but only polynomial in $\alpha$, the condition number, and the accuracy.
\end{itemize}

In short, these primitives are exponential-in-dimension, not unconditional: the
advantage is realized end to end only when structured inputs feed a $\polylog(N)$
computation whose output is a measurable quantity---as in the linear-system solver
(\cref{ch:lec10}), Hamiltonian simulation (\cref{ch:lec11}), and ground-state
energy estimation (\cref{ch:lec05}). Reading the fine print on input and output is
what separates a genuine quantum speedup from a mirage.

\exercisesection
\begin{exercise}\label{ex:lec06-1}
\emph{Norm constraints on a block.} Let $U_A$ be an exact $(\alpha,a)$-block-encoding
of $A$. Show that $\norm{A}\le\alpha$, so that $\alpha\ge\norm{A}$ is necessary.
Conversely exhibit, for any $A$ with $\norm{A}\le\alpha$, a $(1,\cdot)$-block-encoding
of $A/\alpha$ and hence an $(\alpha,\cdot)$-block-encoding of $A$
(cf.\ \cref{prop:lec06-svd}). Explain why $\norm{A}_{\max}\le1$ does \emph{not} by
itself imply $\norm{A}\le1$, and give a $2\times2$ example where
$\norm{A}=s\,\norm{A}_{\max}$ for $s=2$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ex:lec06-2}
\emph{Success probability and subnormalization.} Let $U_A$ be an
$(\alpha,a)$-block-encoding of $A$ and $\ket{b}$ an $n$-qubit state. Show that
applying $U_A$ to $\ket{0^a}\ket{b}$ and measuring the flag register yields $\ket{0^a}$
with probability $p=\norm{A\ket{b}}^2/\alpha^2$, and that conditioned on this outcome
the system register holds $A\ket{b}/\norm{A\ket{b}}$. Deduce how the expected number
of repetitions needed to see the flag once scales with $\alpha$, and comment on the
practical cost of an overly large $\alpha$ (cf.\ \cref{exa:lec06-2x2}).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ex:lec06-3}
\emph{Verifying a $1$-sparse encoding.} Let $A$ be the $4\times4$ matrix with
$A_{c(j),j}=a_j$ in row $c(j)=(j+1)\bmod4$ and zeros elsewhere, where
$a_0,\dots,a_3\in[0,1]$. Write down the oracles $O_c$ and $O_A$ of
\eqref{eq:lec06-Oc1}--\eqref{eq:lec06-OA}, form $U_A=(\id\otimes O_c)O_A$, and verify
directly that $\bra{0,i}U_A\ket{0,j}=A_{ij}$ for all $i,j$, confirming
$U_A\in\mathrm{BE}_{1,1}(A)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ex:lec06-4}
\emph{A Hermitian matrix with a non-Hermitian encoding.} Take the Hermitian diagonal
matrix $A=\diag(a_0,a_1)$ with $a_0\neq a_1$ in $[0,1]$, and its $(1,1)$-block-encoding
$U_A=O_A$ from \cref{exa:lec06-diag} (a controlled rotation). Write $U_A$ as an
explicit $4\times4$ matrix and show that $U_A\neq U_A\dg$ in general, even though $A$
is Hermitian. Explain in one sentence why this motivates the separate notion of a
Hermitian block encoding (\cref{def:lec06-hbe}).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ex:lec06-5}
\emph{The sparsity factor $\alpha=s$.} In the proof of \cref{thm:lec06-ssparse}, track
where each factor of $1/\sqrt s$ arises. Show that the two diffusion operators are
jointly responsible for the block being $A/s$ rather than $A$, and conclude that any
construction based on \eqref{eq:lec06-Uss} has subnormalization $\alpha=s$. What is the
block-encoding factor of a dense matrix ($s=N$) built this way, and why does this make
the construction useless for unstructured dense matrices?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ex:lec06-6}
\emph{An LCU block encoding of a Pauli sum.} Let $A=\tfrac12(\Xgate+\Zgate)$ on a
single qubit. Using \cref{exa:lec06-lcu} with $L=2$, $\beta_0=\beta_1=\tfrac12$,
$V_0=\Xgate$, $V_1=\Zgate$, write down $\mathrm{PREP}$ (a single-qubit rotation on the
one flag qubit), $\mathrm{SEL}$ (a two-qubit controlled gate applying $\Xgate$ or
$\Zgate$), and verify that
$U=(\mathrm{PREP}\dg\otimes\id)\mathrm{SEL}(\mathrm{PREP}\otimes\id)$ is a
$(1,1)$-block-encoding of $A$. What is $\norm{A}$, and is $\alpha=1$ optimal here?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ex:lec06-7}
\emph{Encoding a Hermitian matrix on the unit circle.} Let $A\in\C^{N\times N}$
($N=2^n$) be Hermitian with entries on the complex unit circle,
$A_{ij}=\ee^{\ii\theta_{ij}}$, $\theta_{ij}\in[0,2\pi)$, accessible through a
$2n$-qubit unitary $V$ with
\[
  V\ket{0^n}\ket{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\ee^{\ii\theta_{ij}/2}\ket{i}\ket{j},
  \qquad j\in[N].
\]
Use $V$ to build a block encoding $U$ of $A$ with $n$ ancilla qubits (hint: the
target register should be processed by a mirror-image copy of $V$, exploiting
$\theta_{ji}=-\theta_{ij}$ from Hermiticity), and determine the subnormalization
factor $\alpha$.
\end{exercise}
