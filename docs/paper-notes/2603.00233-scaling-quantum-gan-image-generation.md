# Paper note — Scaling Quantum Machine Learning without Tricks: Full-Resolution and Diverse Image Generation

- **arXiv:** 2603.00233 (the shorthand "2603.0233" redirects here), quant-ph / cs.CV / cs.LG.
- **Authors:** Jonas Jäger, Florian J. Kiwit, Carlos A. Riofrío.
- **Version:** submitted 27 Feb 2026; v2 revised 7 Jul 2026.
- **Course relevance:** a concrete case study for **Lecture 20 (Quantum Machine Learning)** — variational circuits, data encoding, barren plateaus, and generative models — and it uses ideas from Part IV (tensor-network structure of images) and Part I (amplitude encoding, measurement/shots).

---

## TL;DR (one paragraph, plain words)

Most "quantum" image generators cheat: they shrink the image with a classical
neural net first, or chop the image into little patches and use a separate small
quantum circuit for each patch. That makes it unclear whether the quantum part is
doing any real work. This paper builds **one quantum circuit that generates a whole
image at full resolution, for all classes at once, with no such tricks**, and trains
it like a GAN (generator vs. critic) against a normal classical neural-network critic.
The two ingredients that make it work are (1) a circuit **designed to match how images
are stored in qubits** (a task-specific "inductive bias") and (2) a **flexible,
multi-mode noise input** that lets one model produce many distinct-looking samples.
On the standard MNIST and Fashion-MNIST datasets it produces recognizable, varied
images for all ten classes and clearly beats the previous best "patch" quantum GAN.
The authors are careful: everything is **simulated**, and they **do not claim to beat
classical models** — it is a proof that quantum generators can scale to real datasets.

---

## 1. The problem they attack

- **Quantum generative modeling** = use a quantum circuit to learn a data distribution
  and then sample new examples from it (the quantum cousin of GANs / diffusion models).
- Until now it has been stuck on **toy problems**: a few classes, tiny/downscaled images.
- Two "**tricks**" people use to fake scalability, both of which offload the hard part to
  classical computing:
  1. **Dimensionality reduction** — squeeze the image into a small latent vector with
     PCA or an autoencoder, let a small quantum model work there, then a classical net
     blows it back up. (A classical net may be doing most of the generation.)
  2. **Patch generation** — split the image into strips/patches, one small quantum
     generator per patch. (Previous state of the art: Tsang et al., one generator per row.)
- Because of these tricks, prior results were low quality (scattered pixels, classes
  bleeding into each other) and it was **unclear if the quantum model mattered**.

**Their goal:** remove the tricks — a *single, end-to-end* quantum generator that outputs
the full image — and see how far honest quantum generation can go.

## 2. Background you need (plain words)

- **Amplitude encoding:** put a data vector (e.g. pixel values) into the amplitudes of a
  quantum state. `2^A` numbers fit in `A` qubits — exponentially compact — but the state
  must be normalized, so only *relative* values survive (absolute brightness is lost).
- **FRQI encoding (Flexible Representation of Quantum Images):** the encoding they actually
  use. Split the qubits into **address qubits** (which pixel) and one **color qubit** (that
  pixel's gray value stored as an angle in the color qubit). A `2^A`-pixel image needs
  `n = A + 1` qubits. For color images they use the extended **MCRQI** version. This fixes
  the brightness/normalization problem that plain amplitude encoding has.
- **Morton / Z-order pixel indexing:** they number pixels so that the top address bits pick
  the image quadrant, the next bits the sub-quadrant, etc. This hierarchical ordering makes
  the quantum state of a natural image **less entangled** and hence easier to build with a
  shallow circuit. (This is the same "images have low-rank / tensor-network structure"
  insight from the tensor-network lectures.)
- **GAN / Wasserstein GAN (WGAN-GP):** a *generator* tries to make fake samples that fool a
  *discriminator/critic*; the critic tries to tell real from fake. Plain GANs are unstable
  and suffer **mode collapse** (the generator outputs only a few kinds of images). The
  **Wasserstein GAN with gradient penalty (WGAN-GP)** replaces the yes/no critic with a
  smooth "how real is this" score and adds a constraint (1-Lipschitz via a gradient penalty)
  for stable training. They use WGAN-GP throughout.

## 3. The method (their two key ideas)

They keep the **generator quantum** and the **discriminator classical** (a convolutional
neural network), which is standard for classical-data tasks. Training is WGAN-GP. The novel
part is the generator, built from **two complementary "inductive biases"** (i.e., structure
baked into the model that suits the task):

**Idea 1 — a task-specific circuit ansatz aligned with FRQI.** The generator circuit:
1. starts with Hadamards → a uniform superposition (a "uniform gray image" FRQI state);
2. repeats `L` layers, each with (a) **noise-upload** `Rx` rotations, (b) **entangling
   ladders** between address qubits that alternate nearest-neighbor (N2) and
   next-nearest-neighbor (N3) links — which, thanks to Morton order, mix the right spatial
   scales/directions, and (c) **controlled `Ry` rotations on the color qubit** to paint pixel
   values. This structure "knows" about pixels, colors, and spatial scale — unlike the generic
   "hardware-efficient" circuits prior work used.
3. The measured basis-state probabilities are **decoded back into an image** (normalize, then
   invert the sine/cosine to read off each pixel's gray value).

**Idea 2 — tuned multimodal noise.** Real images are *multimodal* (e.g., a central pixel is
usually either black or white, not gray). So instead of one Gaussian blob of input noise, they
sample from **`M` modes** with **learnable centers `μ` and widths `σ`** (a "noise-tuning"
mechanism, realized by classically-controlled rotation layers). This lets a single model spread
its output over many distinct looks and **prevents classes from blending together**.

## 4. What they found (results)

- **Full-resolution, all-class generation:** large models (up to **64 layers, 40 noise modes**,
  ~50,000 training updates) generate all ten classes of **MNIST and Fashion-MNIST** with good
  quality and real intra-class variety (e.g., thin sandal straps).
- **Color proof-of-principle:** extended to **SVHN** color house-number images (restricted to
  digit "0"), showing the approach handles multi-channel data.
- **Ablations — what actually matters:**
  - **Task-specific ansatz ≫ generic ansatz.** The generic ("hardware-efficient") circuit only
    produces vague blobs and **mode-collapses** (drops classes). The task-specific one gives
    sharp edges and covers all classes. The quality gain is **from the design, not just bigger
    models** (a shallow `L=8` task-specific model already beats the prior baseline).
  - **FRQI > plain amplitude encoding** for contrast/saturation and edge sharpness.
  - **Tuned multimodal noise** removes the class-blending/morphing artifacts of single-mode noise.
  - **"Overmoding"** (more noise modes than classes) *improves both diversity and quality*
    — e.g., separating flat vs. heeled boots, short vs. long dresses (analogous to
    classical over-parameterization helping).
- **Shot noise helps (!):** with a finite number of measurement **shots** (e.g., 2048), the
  sampled pixel distribution is *smoother* and avoids systematically dropping dim pixels that
  exact simulation would starve. Training *with* shot noise makes the model transferable to
  real, finite-measurement devices.
- **Beats the prior quantum baseline (patch-QGAN, Tsang et al.):** lower (better) **FID**:
  - MNIST (3-class): **FID 152 vs. 207**.
  - Fashion-MNIST (2-class): **FID 60 vs. 179**.
- **Context vs. classical WGAN-GP** (they stress this is *context*, not a fairness claim):
  classical mean FID ≈ 55 (MNIST) / 85 (Fashion-MNIST); theirs ≈ **109 (MNIST, worse)** but
  **70 (Fashion-MNIST, better than the classical mean)**.
- **Resource contrast:** their generators use only **11–13 qubits** and ~**10,000 parameters**,
  vs. classical models' hundreds of thousands to millions of parameters — a striking
  expressivity-per-parameter contrast (though not a like-for-like comparison).

## 5. Honesty / limitations (what they explicitly do NOT claim)

- **Simulation only** — no run on real quantum hardware.
- **No quantum-advantage claim.** Classical comparisons are "for context"; rigorous
  benchmarking, big hyperparameter searches, and asymptotic-scaling proofs are left as
  future work.
- **Measurement cost:** reading out an `N`-pixel image needs `O(N)` shots. They argue this is
  *not* an exponential blow-up in the problem size (number of pixels) — it's exponential only
  in the number of qubits `n`, and `n = O(log N)` for FRQI — but it is still a real practical
  cost. They sketch fixes: classical/quantum compressed sensing, or measuring in the Fourier
  (QFT) domain.
- **FID caveats:** FID is imperfect; they also use IS/MMD and appendix analyses.
- Trainability (gradient scaling with image size, **barren-plateau** risk) is probed in an
  appendix, not fully resolved.

## 6. Why it's interesting for this course

- A clean, current example of **variational quantum ML** end-to-end: data **encoding** choice
  (FRQI, Morton order) *matters as much as the circuit*, and **inductive bias** is the lever —
  exactly the QML lecture's themes.
- Reinforces the **tensor-network view** (Part IV): natural images are low-entanglement, so
  short circuits can prepare them.
- Concrete touchpoints with Part I: **amplitude encoding**, **measurement/Born rule**, and
  **finite-shot statistics** (here, shots even *help*).
- Good discussion fodder: *where is the quantum model actually doing the work, and how would
  we ever claim advantage?*

## 7. Mini-glossary

- **FRQI / MCRQI:** (multi-channel) flexible representation of quantum images — address qubits
  index the pixel, a color qubit stores the value as an angle.
- **Amplitude encoding:** store a normalized data vector in state amplitudes; `2^A` values in `A` qubits.
- **Inductive bias:** structure built into a model that suits the task (here, an FRQI-aware circuit
  + multimodal noise).
- **WGAN-GP:** Wasserstein GAN with gradient penalty — stable GAN training with a smooth critic.
- **Mode collapse:** generator produces too few kinds of samples (misses classes/variety).
- **Overmoding:** using more latent noise modes than data classes — found to help.
- **Shots:** number of repeated measurements used to estimate output probabilities.
- **FID / IS / MMD:** standard scores for image-generation quality/diversity (lower FID = better).
- **Barren plateau:** vanishing gradients that make large variational circuits hard to train.

## 8. Bottom line

The paper's real contribution is a **methodological one**: drop the classical crutches, and a
*single* quantum GAN generator can produce full-resolution, diverse images for all classes of
standard datasets — **if** you (a) match the circuit to the FRQI image encoding and (b) feed it
tuned multimodal noise. It resets the "honest" baseline for quantum image generation and shows
that finite-shot noise is a feature, not just a bug — while being upfront that hardware runs and
any quantum-advantage question remain open.
